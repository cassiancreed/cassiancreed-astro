By Cassian Creed | Neural Edge Publishing

Colleen Slemmer was a student at the Job Corps Center in Knoxville, Tennessee. She was killed on January 12, 1995. Christa Pike, a fellow student, was convicted of her first-degree murder. This article is about what comes next in that case. It starts with Colleen.

**Status as of Oct 16, 2026 (Pacific):** Christa Pike stands convicted of first-degree murder in the killing of Colleen Slemmer. She remains under a sentence of death. Tennessee's attempt to execute her on Sept. 30, 2026 failed. ABC News reported on Oct. 10 that she is back in prison. No new execution date has been set. The state has not said whether it will try again. [Follow the case on our Court Calendar.](/court-calendar/#pike-execution)

> **Quick answer:** Nothing has been decided yet. Tennessee tried to execute Christa Pike on Sept. 30, 2026, and the attempt failed. Gov. Bill Lee ordered an outside review and paused the state's remaining execution this year. No new date is set. A 1947 Supreme Court case allowed a second try in Louisiana. Experts told the Associated Press it is unclear whether that case still controls.

## Who Colleen Slemmer Was

Colleen Slemmer was a student in the Job Corps program in Knoxville. She was killed on the University of Tennessee's agricultural campus, according to the Tennessee Department of Correction (TDOC).

The legal fight now is about the sentence. It does not change what happened to her.

## Short Timeline

- **Jan. 12, 1995:** Colleen Slemmer is killed in Knoxville. Pike was 18, according to the Associated Press.
- **1996:** Pike is sentenced to death, according to TDOC. A Knox County jury had convicted her of first-degree murder, according to the Tennessee Supreme Court.
- **Oct. 5, 1998:** The Tennessee Supreme Court upholds the conviction and the death sentence.
- **Sept. 28, 2026:** Gov. Lee says he will not intervene on Pike's clemency request, WATE reported.
- **Sept. 30, 2026:** The U.S. Supreme Court lifts a lower court's pause on the execution. Three justices dissent. That night, the attempt to execute Pike fails.
- **Oct. 1, 2026:** Gov. Bill Lee orders an outside review. He says the state's remaining execution this year will not happen.
- **Oct. 3, 2026:** The governor's office names former U.S. Attorney Ed Stanton to lead the review. The prisons chief, Frank Strada, says he will step down.
- **Oct. 7, 2026:** A Nashville judge orders the state to keep all evidence from the failed attempt.
- **Oct. 10, 2026:** Pike's lawyers ask the governor to change her sentence to life without parole. ABC News reports she is back in prison.

## What Officials Said About Sept. 30

We use only official words here. We do not guess about what went wrong.

**The Supreme Court.** On Sept. 30, the U.S. Supreme Court granted Tennessee's request in *Nelsen v. Pike*, No. 26A428. It lifted a pause set by the Sixth Circuit appeals court. Justice Sonia Sotomayor dissented. Justices Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson joined her. Her dissent said Pike had challenges to her conviction that were still pending.

**The prison system.** TDOC said it "followed every step of the State's lawful, established execution protocol approved by the Attorney General's Office." It also said "the protocol does not allow for additional procedures beyond what was carried out this evening." TDOC said Pike was taken to an outside medical facility. These statements were reported by the Tennessee Lookout and Newsweek.

**The governor.** Gov. Lee said, "I have ordered a comprehensive, third-party review to determine exactly what occurred." He also said, "the remaining scheduled execution will not be carried out this year." That was reported by WZTV/FOX 17 and republished by KATV on Oct. 1.

The execution that was paused is Gary Wayne Sutton's. It had been set for Dec. 3, according to Action News 5 on Oct. 3. The governor's office said the review "will seek to determine whether the protocol was followed." It will also look at whether more changes are needed. The Attorney General's office hired Stanton, the governor's office said.

**What has not been said.** The governor's words were about the rest of 2026. They were not described as a formal reprieve for Pike. No end date for the review has been announced. We did not find a public statement from the Attorney General about the attempt.

## What the Law Says

**The 1947 case.** The U.S. Supreme Court case experts point to is the Willie Francis case: *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber* (1947).

Willie Francis was convicted of murder in Louisiana. He was sentenced to die in the electric chair. On May 3, 1946, the switch was thrown. The opinion says that, "presumably because of some mechanical difficulty, death did not result." Louisiana then issued a new death warrant.

The Court voted 5–4 to let Louisiana try again. The lead opinion said three main things:

1. **No double jeopardy.** A second try was not like punishing someone twice for the same crime. The opinion said that when "an accident, with no suggestion of malevolence" stops a sentence, the state's next step is not changed by that.
2. **Not cruel and unusual.** The opinion said the "unforeseeable accident" could not "add an element of cruelty to a subsequent execution."
3. **Officials were assumed careful.** The Court said nothing showed the officials acted wrongly. It wrote, "Accidents happen for which no man is to blame."

Four justices dissented.

**Does that case still control?** Experts are not sure. The Associated Press asked them on Oct. 7, 2026. They said they know of no state or federal law that clearly bans a second attempt. But death penalty scholar Jordan Steiker said it is unclear whether the 1947 ruling would be treated as controlling. He noted the Court has not revisited it under its modern test, which looks at "evolving standards of decency." Lawyer John Mills told AP a second attempt would "raise a strong claim" under the Eighth Amendment, which bans cruel and unusual punishment.

There are also key differences. The 1947 case involved the electric chair. Pike's case involved lethal injection. The 1947 Court also called its case an accident. Why the Sept. 30 attempt failed has not been officially explained. We did not find a Tennessee law or court ruling that answers this question. No court has ruled on how the 1947 case applies to Pike, as far as we found by Oct. 16.

## What Happens Next

There are four paths to watch. None has a public end date.

1. **The outside review.** Ed Stanton is leading it. No deadline has been announced. Its findings could shape what the state does next.
2. **The Nashville court case.** Pike's lawyers filed suit in Davidson County Chancery Court. The case is *Pike v. Skrmetti*, No. 26-0027-III. On Oct. 7, Chancellor I'Ashea Myles ordered the state to preserve evidence from the attempt, according to the Death Penalty Information Center. That includes drugs, equipment, recordings and records. Next court date: not announced.
3. **The request to the governor.** Gov. Lee declined to intervene on a clemency request on Sept. 28, before the attempt, WATE reported. After the attempt, her lawyers asked him to commute her sentence to life without parole, ABC News reported on Oct. 10. That is a request outside the courts. We found no public answer from the governor to the new request.
4. **Other challenges.** Justice Sotomayor's dissent said Pike had pending challenges to her conviction. Their next steps have not been announced.

**Will Tennessee try again?** Not announced. The governor's office did not answer AP's question about that, AP reported on Oct. 7. We will not guess.

**Is there a new execution date?** No date has been set.
