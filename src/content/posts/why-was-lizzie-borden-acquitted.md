*By Cassian Creed*

Andrew and Abby Borden were killed in their Fall River home on August 4, 1892. The next year, a jury found Andrew's daughter Lizzie Borden not guilty. Why? The prosecution had a serious circumstantial case, but important links remained open to reasonable doubt. No witness saw Lizzie attack either victim. No bloodied weapon or clothing was tied to her. Her disputed statements could look suspicious, but they were not direct proof. The judge said opportunity, suspicion, and even strong probability were not enough.

That answer does not mean there was “no case.” It also does not mean the verdict proved Lizzie was innocent. It means the jury was not persuaded of guilt beyond reasonable doubt on the evidence it was allowed to consider.

## The prosecution's strongest case

The Commonwealth's best case was a chain of opportunity, alleged false stories, and possible concealment.

**Evidence:** Bridget Sullivan, the family's maid, saw no outsider enter while she worked around the house. After Andrew came home, Bridget went upstairs. About 10 to 15 minutes after eleven, Lizzie called her down and said someone had killed her father. Bridget had also heard Lizzie laugh upstairs. This placed Lizzie at the house and gave her a clear chance to act.

**Argument:** The prosecution said Lizzie's stories hid the crimes. Lizzie said a note called Abby to someone who was sick, but no note, sender, or messenger was found. She also said she had been in the barn. The state used the heat and dust to challenge that account. Its theory was that one story explained Abby's absence and the other placed Lizzie away from Andrew's killing.

**Evidence:** Alice Russell testified that two days after the killings, she saw Lizzie burn a dress in the kitchen stove. Lizzie said it was an old dress covered with paint.

**Argument:** The state framed the burning as destruction of evidence. It used property tension and Lizzie's remarks the day before the killings to suggest motive or foreknowledge.

Together, these points formed a real case. But each step required jurors to choose the prosecution's meaning over another possible meaning.

## Where the chain could break

The first problem was presence. The judge told jurors that, under the state's theory that Lizzie personally killed Andrew, her presence in the house at that moment was essential. Opportunity alone could not support a conviction.

**Evidence:** Bridget's work took her outside, and she was unsure whether a screen door was locked. Hymon Lubinsky saw an unidentified woman moving from the barn direction toward the rear steps shortly after eleven. He could not identify her or say she entered.

**Unknown:** The trial record did not settle exactly where Lizzie was at the instant Andrew was attacked.

The second problem was physical proof.

**Evidence:** Tests found no blood on the examined axes or claw-hammer hatchet. Dr. David Cheever testified that a person attacking Andrew from behind could have received significant blood spatter. Adelaide Churchill, who saw Lizzie soon after the alarm, noticed no blood on her dress, hands, or face and no disorder in her hair.

**Unknown:** The state did not identify the murder weapon or prove what the killer wore. No blood did not make Lizzie's guilt impossible. A different weapon, position, or clothes were possible, but each added an unproved step.

The third problem was that suspicious conduct can have more than one explanation.

**Argument:** The missing note could suggest invention, but it did not prove no note existed. The barn account could have been false, but that was not certain. The dress burning could suggest concealment, yet Russell said it happened openly while police were outside. She saw no blood on it.

The judge warned jurors to treat reported speech carefully. A witness can misunderstand, misremember, or change a phrase while repeating it. Several parts of the case depended on what witnesses recalled Lizzie saying.

## What the jury did not hear

Later retellings often mix the full Borden legend with the evidence admitted at trial. They are not the same.

The jury did not receive Lizzie's inquest testimony.

The poison claim needs care. The court would consider an alleged prussic-acid purchase if the prosecution laid its promised foundation. That failed. Eli Bence did not identify Lizzie to the jury as the buyer. Because jurors heard some surrounding testimony, it is too broad to say all poison evidence was excluded.

**Unknown:** The official transcript does not preserve the judge's exact final reason in a single quoted ruling. What the record does show is that the identifying purchase allegation never reached the jury.

## Why reasonable doubt controlled the result

The judge said circumstantial cases are open to error because jurors must decide both which facts are true and what they mean. The Commonwealth carried the full burden. Lizzie had to prove nothing, and jurors could not hold her silence against her.

The question was not whether Lizzie looked suspicious. It was whether every essential link pointed to guilt with moral certainty. The judge used the blood evidence as an example: if the killer would have been spattered, Lizzie had no blood on her, and there was no time to clean up, certainty could fail.

The jury returned a confirmed verdict of not guilty. The record does not tell us which gap mattered most to each juror. The fairest answer is not that there was no case against Lizzie Borden. It is that the case the jury was legally allowed to decide did not compel guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

Andrew and Abby should not disappear behind the Lizzie legend. The trial record restores them to the story and shows why careful evidence labels matter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why was Lizzie Borden found not guilty?**

The circumstantial case left doubt about Lizzie's location, the weapon, blood evidence, and her disputed conduct. The judge said opportunity, suspicion, and strong probability were not enough.

**Was poison evidence used at Lizzie Borden's trial?**

Jurors heard surrounding prussic-acid testimony, but not Eli Bence identifying Lizzie as the buyer. The prosecution failed to complete the foundation for that allegation.

**Did the jury hear Lizzie Borden's inquest testimony?**

No. The court excluded it from the prosecution's trial case. Its later public availability does not make it trial evidence.
