Two kinds of court news raise the same questions again and again. One is a trial that ends without a verdict. The other is a verdict that says "not guilty by reason of insanity." This page answers five questions readers ask most, in plain words, with the law behind each answer.

Two live cases appear below as examples. In both, every charge is an allegation, and the person charged is presumed innocent. Nothing here predicts what any court will decide.

## Do you go free after a mistrial?

No. A mistrial is not a verdict. No one has been convicted, and no one has been acquitted. The charges stay in place, and prosecutors decide what happens next: retry the case, offer a plea, or drop it.

For families who have waited years for answers, a mistrial means waiting longer. It is not a verdict in either direction.

A mistrial also does not wipe out custody or bail. Those are decided by the judge, and a mistrial does not change them on its own.

**Example:** On September 4, 2026, Judge William Sullivan declared a mistrial in the Lindsay Clancy case after the jury said it could not reach a unanimous verdict. No acquittal or conviction was entered. She remains charged with three counts of first-degree murder, has pleaded not guilty, and is presumed innocent. On November 16, 2026, the judge set the retrial for March 29, 2027, according to CBS Boston and NBC Boston. A trial date is a schedule, not a finding about the charges. ## Can you be retried if you have a mistrial?

Usually, yes. The Fifth Amendment says a person cannot be tried twice for the same offense. But the U.S. Supreme Court has held that a mistrial caused by a hung jury does not end the first trial's "jeopardy," so the government may try the case again. That rule comes from *Richardson v. United States* (1984).

There is a narrow exception. If a defendant asks for a mistrial because of something the prosecutor did, a retrial is barred only when the prosecutor meant to provoke that request. That is the rule from *Oregon v. Kennedy* (1982). Sloppy or even improper conduct is not enough on its own under that federal rule.

State law can add protections. Some states' own constitutions have gone further than the federal rule, so the answer in a particular state depends on that state's courts.

## Is NGRI considered an acquittal?

The verdict is "not guilty," but it is not the same as a finding that the person did not do it. That is why "NGRI" (not guilty by reason of insanity) is so often misunderstood.

An NGRI verdict means the jury found that mental illness left the person legally unable to be held responsible for the act. It does not erase the act. It also does not mean release. In *Jones v. United States* (1983), the Supreme Court held that the Constitution lets the government commit a person found not guilty by reason of insanity to a mental hospital, and that the commitment can last until the person is no longer mentally ill or no longer dangerous. That can be longer than a prison sentence for the same crime would have been.

In Massachusetts, the defense is formally called "lack of criminal responsibility," and the verdict triggers a commitment process rather than release. Our full explainer covers it: ["Not Criminally Responsible" Does Not Mean Walking Free](/post/ngri-massachusetts-explainer/).

## What does NGRI mean in court terms?

NGRI stands for "not guilty by reason of insanity." Some states say "not guilty by reason of mental illness," and Massachusetts says "lack of criminal responsibility." "Insanity" here is a legal word, not a medical one. Doctors diagnose illnesses. Courts decide whether an illness met a legal test at the time of the act.

Three things to keep straight:

1. **A diagnosis is not a defense.** Having a serious mental illness does not, by itself, meet the legal test. The illness must have affected the person's capacity at the time of the act in the way the state's standard requires.
2. **The test differs by state.** In Massachusetts, the standard asks whether mental disease or defect left the person without substantial capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of the conduct or to conform it to the law.
3. **The burden differs by place.** In Massachusetts, once the defense is raised, the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the person was criminally responsible. Other places set the burden differently.

## The one idea behind all five answers

Each answer turns on a single question: has the criminal case reached an end point? Legal writers call the start of a trial the moment "jeopardy attaches." A verdict of guilty or not guilty ends it. A hung jury does not, which is why a mistrial pauses a case instead of closing it. An NGRI verdict ends the path to punishment, but it opens a separate path to hospital commitment. A hearing date, finally, is only a schedule. It can move.

When you read a headline about any of these, ask which of those four things actually happened. Most confusion comes from mixing them up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a mistrial mean the defendant is innocent?**
No. A mistrial means the trial ended without a verdict. It says nothing about guilt or innocence.

**Can prosecutors drop the case after a mistrial?**
Yes. Prosecutors can decide not to retry the case. That is a separate decision from the mistrial itself.

**Does NGRI mean the person walks free?**
No. The verdict usually leads to commitment to a mental health facility, and release depends on a later court finding.

**Are the Lindsay Clancy and Tyler Robinson dates final?**
No. Court dates can move. Check the Court Calendar for the latest verified entries.
