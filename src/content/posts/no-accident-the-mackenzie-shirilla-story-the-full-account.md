At first, it was an accident. In the pre-dawn dark of July 31, 2022, a black Toyota Camry left an industrial road in Strongsville, Ohio, crossed an intersection, and struck the corner of a building. Two young men in the car — Dominic Russo, twenty, and Davion Flanagan, nineteen — were killed instantly. The seventeen-year-old driver, Russo's girlfriend, survived. Nearly everyone who saw the scene saw the same thing: a terrible, ordinary tragedy. They were wrong.

## The anomaly that wouldn't resolve

There were no skid marks. The car's event-data recorder told a stranger story still: in the moments before impact it was traveling near 99 miles per hour with the accelerator pressed to the floor — and the brake never touched. A car does not drive itself into a wall at full throttle by accident. The county medical examiner amended the manner of death for both young men from accident to homicide, and an event the world had filed under misfortune became something that demanded an explanation.

## A rehearsal in the data

Investigators found one. Location data showed that the obscure route to the crash site had been driven days earlier — a path the driver had no ordinary reason to take. The state would call it a dry run. A medical emergency does not scout its own route three days in advance; planning and accident are mutually exclusive. Mackenzie Shirilla was arrested in November 2022 and, though she was a minor at the time of the crash, charged as an adult.

## The trial

She chose a bench trial — a judge, not a jury. On August 14, 2023, she was found guilty on twelve felony counts, including two counts of murder. The judge described the driving as "controlled, methodical, deliberate, intentional and purposeful," and accepted the prosecution's theory: a survived murder-suicide, rooted in a relationship the driver could not accept losing. Davion Flanagan, on that account, was simply present — a friend in the back seat, and his death its own crime, with its own life sentence attached.

## The defense, and the gap at its center

The defense offered an innocent explanation for the one most incriminating fact: the absence of braking. It argued that a medical episode — postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, which can cause fainting — may have left the driver unconscious at the wheel. It is a real condition, and as a hypothesis it is not absurd. But at trial it was unsupported by the proof such a claim requires, and it could not answer the evidence beside it: an unconscious driver brakes nothing, but neither does she hold an accelerator to the floor for four hundred feet. The court weighed data over hypothesis.

## Sentence, and what is still open

On August 21, 2023, Shirilla received two life sentences, to be served concurrently, with parole eligibility after fifteen years. An Ohio appeals court affirmed the convictions. In 2026 a new legal team filed a post-conviction petition arguing that her original lawyer failed to present medical evidence for the POTS theory, with a neurologist now opining that her records are consistent with a medical episode; an earlier appeal was rejected after it was filed a day late, and the state's highest court has not said whether it will hear the case. The conviction stands as the established fact it is. The challenge is real, ongoing, and — like every legal question — one the courts, not a documentary or a book, will decide.
