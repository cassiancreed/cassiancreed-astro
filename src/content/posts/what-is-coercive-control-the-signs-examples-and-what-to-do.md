If you are in immediate physical danger or need urgent medical help in the United States, call **911** or local emergency services when it is safe to do so. For suicide, mental-health, or substance-use crisis support, call or text **988**. These are different services: 988 does not replace 911 for immediate physical danger.

The **National Domestic Violence Hotline** offers free, confidential advocate support 24/7 at **800-799-SAFE (7233)**, by texting **START to 88788**, or through [TheHotline.org](https://www.thehotline.org/get-help/). No NEP signup is required to use any crisis or survivor resource.

## What coercive control means

Coercive control describes a pattern of acts or behaviors used to take away another person’s freedom and control daily life. It may involve fear, pressure, shame, rules, monitoring, isolation, financial restrictions, threats, or other tactics. Physical violence may be present, but a pattern can be harmful even when no single incident captures it. This description follows current guidance from [WomensLaw](https://www.womenslaw.org/about-abuse/forms-abuse/emotional-and-psychological-abuse/forms-emotional-and-psychological-abuse-0) and the National Domestic Violence Hotline’s [power-and-control framework](https://www.thehotline.org/identify-abuse/power-and-control/).

This is a behavioral description, not a diagnosis and not a prediction. Laws use different definitions in different places. For location-specific legal information, consult a qualified local professional or use [WomensLaw’s state-by-state legal information](https://www.womenslaw.org/laws).

## Recognize a pattern, not a single label

Possible tactics can include:

- monitoring a phone, location, accounts, messages, or contacts;
- limiting access to money, transportation, work, education, healthcare, or identification documents;
- isolating someone from friends, family, community, or support;
- using children, pets, housing, immigration status, disability, employment, or private information as leverage;
- making threats, setting one-sided rules, humiliating, intimidating, or punishing independence; or
- repeatedly ignoring boundaries and then minimizing the behavior or shifting responsibility.

One item does not prove a relationship is abusive or establish what will happen next. The useful question is whether a repeated pattern is reducing freedom, increasing fear, or making ordinary choices feel unsafe. Responsibility belongs exclusively to the person choosing control or harm.

## Preserve your options

There is no single correct response. Leaving, reporting, documenting, blocking, changing passwords, or removing location access can carry different risks for different people. A person targeted by abuse is the expert on details outsiders may not see, and may need to act on their own timeline.

Options to consider, when safe and useful, include:

- speaking privately with a trained advocate about the full pattern;
- using a safer device the other person has not accessed;
- identifying one trusted person and a safer way to communicate;
- considering documents, medication, transportation, money, children, pets, work, school, and accessibility needs in a personalized plan; and
- preserving messages or incident details somewhere the other person cannot access, if documentation would not increase danger.

The Hotline provides [personalized safety-planning resources](https://www.thehotline.org/plan-for-safety/). Safety planning is a way to consider options; it is not a guarantee and is not a test a survivor can fail.

## Technology may be part of the pattern

Phones, shared accounts, vehicles, cloud services, family plans, and connected-home devices can expose location or private information. If monitoring is possible, changing settings or removing a tracker can sometimes alert the person who has access. The [NNEDV Safety Net Project technology plan](https://www.techsafety.org/resources-survivors/technology-safety-plan) recommends prioritizing safety and considering documentation before major changes.

## Supporting another person without taking control

Listen without judgment. Ask what would be helpful. Avoid ultimatums or demands that may deepen isolation. A survivor may choose to stay, leave, return, report, not report, or delay action for reasons others cannot see. The Hotline’s guidance for [friends and family](https://www.thehotline.org/resources/safety-planning-for-friends-and-family/) emphasizes support and safety planning rather than taking decisions away from the person affected.

## What legal information can and cannot tell you

Legal definitions, available orders, reporting rules, evidence requirements, and remedies vary by jurisdiction. General online information cannot tell someone whether a specific act meets a legal definition or what response will be safest. [WomensLaw](https://www.womenslaw.org/laws) provides plain-language state information, but individualized questions belong with a qualified local advocate or attorney.
