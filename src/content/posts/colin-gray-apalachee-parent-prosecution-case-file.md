Colin Gray did not fire a shot at Apalachee High School. A Barrow County jury convicted him of murder anyway.

In March 2026, jurors found him guilty on 27 counts arising from the September 4, 2024 shooting carried out by his 14-year-old son — including two counts of second-degree murder, two counts of involuntary manslaughter, 18 counts of cruelty to children in the second degree, and five counts of reckless conduct. The Georgia Bureau of Investigation called it the first time in Georgia history that the parent of a school shooting suspect had been charged.

He is scheduled to be sentenced on **Thursday, July 30, 2026, at 9:00 a.m.** in Barrow County Superior Court. Two days before that hearing, on July 24, his son pleaded guilty to all 55 counts against him and began his own sentencing hearing.

## Why the July 30 Hearing Matters

This is a sentencing, not a trial. The jury has already decided the facts. What is left is how long a father serves for a killing his son committed.

That question has no settled answer, because there is barely any Georgia law on it. The theory prosecutors used — that a parent who supplies a firearm to a child he has reason to believe is dangerous can be criminally liable for what the child then does — had never produced a Georgia murder conviction before this one. Whatever number Judge Primm reads out on July 30 becomes a reference point that prosecutors and defense lawyers in the next case will argue about.

### Why "second-degree murder" here does not mean what most readers think

This is the part almost every account skips, and without it the count structure is unreadable.

Georgia's second-degree murder statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-5-1(d), is narrow and specific: "A person commits the offense of murder in the second degree when, in the commission of cruelty to children in the second degree, he or she causes the death of another human being irrespective of malice."

Second-degree cruelty to children is the *only* predicate. That is why this indictment carries 18 counts of cruelty to children in the second degree sitting alongside two counts of second-degree murder — they are not separate theories, they are the same theory at two grades. The cruelty counts are the engine; the murder counts are what the engine produced where a child died.

It also means second-degree murder in Georgia is not "murder, but less." It is a distinct offense with its own punishment range: **10 to 30 years**, under § 16-5-1(e)(2). Malice murder and felony murder — the counts his son pleaded to — are the ones carrying death, life without parole, or life.

**What that means for the maximum.** Reporting on Colin Gray's total exposure varies: some accounts say more than 100 years, others up to 180, and neither comes with a per-count breakdown. We are not going to assert a total. What we can give you is the framework the judge is working inside — two second-degree murder counts at 10 to 30 years each, two involuntary manslaughter counts, 18 counts of second-degree cruelty to children, and five reckless conduct counts. Whether those run concurrently or consecutively is the entire question, and the judgment entered on July 30 is what will settle it.

## The Four People Who Were Killed

**Mason Alexander Schermerhorn, 14.** A freshman, born in May 2010. He was autistic. He loved Walt Disney World and LEGOs, played Roblox and Kingdom Hearts, and had picked up the trumpet because he wanted to be like his sister Alanna. His favorite color was red, and hundreds of people wore it to his memorial. *He is one of the two decedents named in the second-degree murder counts.*

**Christian Gabriel Angulo, 14.** A freshman, the son of Ismael and Emma Angulo. He liked video games and soccer, and the people who knew him describe someone funny and free-spirited who worked at making other people laugh. Roughly 600 mourners came to his funeral Mass, celebrated in English and Spanish. *He is the other decedent named in the second-degree murder counts.*

**Richard "Ricky" Aspinwall, 39.** A math teacher and the Apalachee Wildcats' defensive coordinator, hired in 2023. His head coach, Mike Hancock, said of him: "He loved being a girl dad, and he loved his wife." He had two young daughters. We are not naming them. His coaching mantra was three words — effort, attitude, trust. *He is one of the two decedents named in the involuntary manslaughter counts.*

**Cristina Irimie, 53.** A math teacher who worked with English-language learners. She left Romania in the mid-to-late 1990s, led a children's traditional dance group at her church, and — having no children of her own — told a relative, "My students are my children." *She is the other decedent named in the involuntary manslaughter counts.*

Nine more people were injured — eight students and one teacher. The 18 counts of cruelty to children in the second degree and the five reckless conduct counts reach that wider harm. Several of the wounded have testified under their own names; others have not, and are not named here.

We have written about each of these four at length in [the Colt Gray case file](/post/colt-gray-apalachee-high-school-case-file/). They are the reason this page exists, and they were people long before they were counts on an indictment.

## What the Jury Heard

The prosecution's case was that Colin Gray had ample warning about his son and armed him anyway.

**The May 2023 tip.** The FBI received anonymous tips about online threats to shoot up an unidentified school, including photographs of guns, and traced them to Georgia within 24 hours. The Jackson County Sheriff's Office interviewed Colt Gray, then 13, and his father at their home on May 21, 2023. Body camera footage later released by the sheriff's office captured Colin Gray saying, "there's nothing loaded. Actually, we do a lot of shooting. We do a lot of deer hunting. He shot his first deer this year," and telling deputies that if his son had made shooting threats he would be "mad as hell, and then all the guns will go away." The report closed as unsubstantiated. No arrest, no referral.

What happened next is disputed, and the dispute matters. The FBI's public statement says Jackson County alerted local schools to monitor the subject. Reporting on the sheriff's own written report indicates it does not document any formal school notification, and families pursuing civil claims allege the school districts were never told. No court has resolved this. We present the notification as the FBI's assertion, not as established fact.

**The rifle.** Colin Gray testified in his own defense that he gave his son a SIG Sauer SIGM400 — the AR-platform rifle used in the shooting — around Christmas in December 2023. He characterized it on the stand as "part of his Christmas" rather than an outright gift, said he retained ownership with title passing at 18 or graduation, and said he told his son, "This is a weapon that I want you to shoot when we go to the range." He testified it was stored in his bedroom closet. Prosecutors introduced an unused gun lock, still in its packaging, found in Colt's closet.

**The mother's testimony.** Marcee Gray testified she had repeatedly urged Colin to secure the firearms — "They need to be locked somewhere. Initially he said he would." She testified their son had told her he needed a tactical vest his father bought him to "finish my school shooter outfit," and that she took it as a joke because he was laughing. She acknowledged his fascination with the Parkland shooter. Colin Gray denied knowing about it.

Colin Gray had himself written of his son that "we have had a very difficult past couple of years and he needs help. Anger, anxiety, quick to be volatile."

Assistant District Attorney Patricia Brooks put the theory to the jury in closing: "the defendant had sufficient warning that his son was a bomb just waiting to go off. And instead of disarming him, he gave him the detonator."

The jury deliberated for just under two hours.

## What This Case Changed

Two years earlier, in Oxford, Michigan, James and Jennifer Crumbley were prosecuted over their son's school shooting. Georgia Public Broadcasting reported that both sides in the Colin Gray trial referenced that case.

Per that same reporting, the Crumbley parents faced **manslaughter** charges. Colin Gray was convicted of **second-degree murder** as to two of the four decedents, plus involuntary manslaughter as to the other two.

Michael Moore, a former U.S. Attorney for the Middle District of Georgia, described the significance to Georgia Public Broadcasting: "This is sort of where the slope of the law is headed, when you basically say that somebody can be responsible for a third party's conduct when they're not part of some type of criminal scheme or conspiracy."

That is the live question. Criminal liability normally requires that you did the thing. Parent-liability prosecutions extend it to failing to prevent someone else from doing the thing — and the boundary of that extension has not been drawn by any appellate court in Georgia. It is the most likely subject of an appeal here, and an appellate opinion in this case would be the first real map.

We are deliberately not laying the two prosecutions side by side count-for-count. We have not independently verified the Michigan record — the exact counts, the sentences imposed, the appellate history — and a comparison is only as good as its weaker half. The charge-level difference above is what one cited source reports; anything past that needs the Michigan docket.

## What to Watch Next

**The number, and how the counts stack.** Whether Judge Primm runs the sentences concurrently or consecutively will matter more than any single count's maximum. This is where the reported "100 years" and "180 years" figures get resolved into an actual judgment.

**Whether a notice of appeal follows.** It cannot be filed until sentence is imposed. If it is, the question presented is likely to be whether Georgia's second-degree murder statute can reach a parent on these facts — the first appellate test of the theory.

**Whether the record addresses the two dropped counts.** They vanished before jury submission with no public explanation. The sentencing record may or may not say why.

**Whether the storage question gets asked anywhere but a courtroom.** The premise of this prosecution is that a rifle given to a teenager at Christmas was in a school nine months later. Georgia has no statute that required that rifle to be secured. That absence is part of why this arrived as a homicide prosecution rather than a storage offense, and it is the part of the case that outlives the sentence.

**The civil claims.** Several families have filed *ante litem* notices against Barrow County Schools, the Jackson County Sheriff's Office, and named officials — the statutory prerequisite to suing a government entity in Georgia. Those are notices, not lawsuits. No court has ruled on any allegation in them, and no suit against Colin Gray personally has been verified.
