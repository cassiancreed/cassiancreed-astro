## 1. HER NAME WAS JANE HART

Jane Hart was born in Ohio in 1906, a year after her mother arrived from Croatia. She grew up at St. Joseph's Orphanage for Girls in Cleveland after her father's death broke the family apart, and she built a quiet, hardworking life as a housekeeper in Chicago's Rogers Park. She was sixty-nine years old when someone took her life — and then took her name, for fifty years.

On October 8, 1976, the harvest season in rural Benton County, Indiana, was interrupted by a discovery that would haunt the community for half a century. Near the small town of Otterbein, a farmer named Norman Skoog was navigating his corn picker through a field when he nearly struck a small cardboard box, abandoned just fifteen yards from the road. Inside, wrapped in plastic, lay the body of a woman who had been shot in the back of the head.

She was found wearing a double-knit pantsuit — a two-tone green jacket and solid green slacks — with a single broken bottle of perfume beside her. Investigators estimated her age between 55 and 65; beyond those sparse details, the record went silent. For nearly fifty years she was known only as "Benton County Jane Doe." In 2021, the DNA Doe Project took on the mission to restore the name that had been taken from her.

## 2. TAKEAWAY 1: IDENTITY IS A FUNDAMENTAL HUMAN RIGHT

The search for Jane Doe was never merely about solving a forensic puzzle; it was an act of restoring human dignity. In 2019, Benton County Coroner Matthew Rosenbarger initiated the exhumation of the victim's remains, a critical step toward applying 21st-century science to a 20th-century tragedy. The effort was driven by the conviction that no person should remain nameless in death.

Traci Onders, a team co-leader with the DNA Doe Project, underscored the moral weight of the investigation:

"Identity is a basic human right. Jane Hart, when she died, somebody went to great lengths to ensure that her identity was taken away from her."

Naming the dead is a critical act of justice. By identifying Jane Hart, investigators did more than update a case file; they pulled a victim out of the clinical shadows of "unidentified remains" and restored her to the human record.

## 3. TAKEAWAY 2: THE "BLIND SPOT" IN GLOBAL DNA DATABASES

The path to Jane Hart's name was blocked by significant technical hurdles. When the DNA Doe Project began building her genetic profile in 2021, they encountered a profound "blind spot": the victim's Croatian ancestry. While investigative genetic genealogy (IGG) has revolutionized the field, its power is limited by the diversity of the databases it queries.

Eastern European populations are currently underrepresented in these systems, making cases involving this heritage exceptionally difficult. At the start of the search, Jane Doe's highest early match shared a mere 1.6% of her DNA—a distant thread that required years of meticulous labor to follow.

Harmony Vollmer, team co-leader, noted the urgency of this digital gap:

"Without more people of Eastern European descent uploading to the GEDmatch.com, FamilyTreeDNA.com and DNAJustice.org databases we have access to, cases like this will remain tricky to solve."

## 4. TAKEAWAY 3: THE TRAGIC HIDDEN HISTORY OF THE HART FAMILY

A breakthrough in 2024 finally reconnected Jane Hart to the world she left behind. Researchers traced her lineage back to a Croatian woman who immigrated to the United States in 1905. Jane was born a year later in Ohio, but her early life was defined by the sudden death of her father. Faced with insurmountable economic hardship, Jane's mother was unable to support all her children. The family was fractured; one son was kept, while a second brother was sent away and Jane was placed in St. Joseph's Orphanage for Girls in Cleveland.

Jane lived in institutions until she was old enough to support herself, eventually moving to Chicago. There, she lived a quiet, hardworking life as a housekeeper in the Rogers Park neighborhood, near the intersection of Paulina and Lunt. Physical evidence from 1976 corroborated this history of labor, noting her "calloused hands."

According to the DNA Doe Project, Jane Hart was 69 years old when she was killed. Perhaps the most tragic revelation was the silence that followed her disappearance. Her surviving relatives, including her 78-year-old niece Mary Kay Ross, had no idea Jane even existed until the DNA Doe Project reached out. A branch of the family tree had been severed and forgotten for decades.

## 5. TAKEAWAY 4: THE "TATTOOED" MEMORY OF A RURAL COMMUNITY

While Jane Hart's life was lived in the bustle of Chicago, her memory remained etched into the landscape of rural Indiana. Curtis Skoog was 16 the day of the discovery, and it never left him — he described it as being "tattooed" in his mind. For a small town where "everybody knew everybody," the unidentified woman was a defining, unresolved grief carried for five decades. The resolution of the case on July 15, 2026, represents a bridge between eras—connecting the "old-fashioned police work" of the 1970s with the cutting-edge technology of the present to finally cross a fifty-year finish line.

## 6. TAKEAWAY 5: A NAME RESTORED, BUT A MYSTERY REMAINS

While the restoration of Jane Hart's name is a victory for her dignity, the legal posture of the case remains complex. The identification was a collaborative success involving the DNA Doe Project and the Benton County Coroner's Office, supported by laboratory partners HudsonAlpha Discovery, Lakehead University's Paleo-DNA Lab, Astrea Forensics, Intermountain Forensics, and bioinformaticist Kevin Lord.

However, giving Jane her name back is not the same as securing a conviction. While the identification provides a vital starting point for law enforcement, public reporting did not establish that her homicide had become an active prosecution. Coroner Rosenbarger has indicated that "additional intelligence would be acted upon," but for now, the investigation into who killed Jane Hart and why she was left in a lonely Indiana field remains an open question.

## 7. CONCLUSION: THE POWER OF THE COLLECTIVE

The identification of Jane Hart after half a century is a testament to the persistence of volunteer genealogists and the evolving capabilities of science. For her niece, Mary Kay Ross, the restoration of Jane's name brings a somber peace and the hope that her aunt is finally at rest.

This case shows what public DNA databases can do when people knowingly choose to participate. Databases like GEDmatch and DNAJustice.org only include profiles their owners have explicitly opted in to sharing — that consent is the foundation the whole method stands on. As those who choose to participate grow more diverse, more families like Jane's may be reunited with relatives they never knew they were missing. Whether to be one of them is a personal decision, and an informed one is the only kind worth making.
