> **If you recognize a concrete detail connected to Nancy Guthrie’s disappearance, send it privately to the FBI at 1-800-CALL-FBI or [tips.fbi.gov](https://tips.fbi.gov/). Do not post names, handwriting accusations, or alleged evidence publicly.**

The release of two early ransom-related communications created a new wave of interest in the Nancy Guthrie case. It also created a predictable problem: readers began treating the documents as though every sentence had already been authenticated.

That is not how evidence works.

A document may be authentic as an object—meaning investigators received it when and where they say they did—without being truthful in content. Its author may know something, repeat something, exaggerate, misdirect, or lie. The task is to separate the existence of the document from the reliability of its claims.

## What do authorities confirm about the communications?

Authorities confirm that media outlets received the communications shortly after Nancy disappeared and that investigators reviewed them. Pima County authorities released two of them on July 31, 2026, hoping someone would recognize distinctive language or another useful detail.

That public release is a lead-generation strategy. It is not a verdict about authorship or truthfulness.

## What did unknown writers claim?

One communication demanded ransom. A later communication claimed that Nancy had died and had been left outdoors.

Those statements must remain attributed to an unknown writer. Investigators have not publicly confirmed Nancy’s death. A responsible headline cannot convert an unverified statement inside a document into an established fact.

## What can investigators examine?

Without speculating about the unreleased evidence in this case, investigators can generally study:

- when and how a communication was sent;
- account, device, network, routing, mail, or delivery information;
- wording, spelling, formatting, and repeated language patterns;
- details that were public versus details that may have been known only to involved people;
- cryptocurrency or payment instructions;
- links between communications and physical or digital evidence;
- whether later claims match independently established facts.

The public does not have access to the full comparison set, chain of custody, forensic results, or investigative interviews. That makes confident online authorship claims unreliable.

## Do the communications prove Nancy Guthrie died?

No public authority cited in this report has confirmed that conclusion. The current FBI material continues to seek Nancy’s location. The correct status is that she remains missing.

This does not mean investigators disregard the statement. It means publishers should not claim more than authorities have established.

## Do the communications prove the writer was involved?

Not by themselves. The writer could be involved, have secondhand knowledge, exploit public reporting, or fabricate the account. Investigators assess the document alongside nonpublic evidence. Readers should not assume the answer.

## Why not publish the complete text everywhere?

Reproducing the documents as spectacle can reward the writer, spread instructions or private details, distort witness memory, and encourage false pattern matching. A source-linked summary usually serves the public better.

The safest editorial method is to quote little or not at all, describe only the necessary claim, and immediately state whether authorities have confirmed it.

## The three-label method

Every statement in coverage should carry one of three mental labels:

### Confirmed

Supported by an official document, agency statement, court record, or multiple reliable sources.

### Claimed

Asserted by an unknown writer, witness, advocate, attorney, family member, or commentator and attributed accurately.

### Unknown

Not established publicly or dependent on evidence investigators have not released.

This method keeps uncertainty visible without pretending the case is unknowable.

## What should people avoid doing?

- Do not name private individuals as suspects.
- Do not publish side-by-side handwriting accusations.
- Do not crowdsource home addresses, phone numbers, workplaces, or family links.
- Do not contact or confront someone you think resembles the person in the video.
- Do not send theories repeatedly to tip lines without a fact-based basis.
- Do not state that Nancy is dead.

## What should a useful tip contain?

A useful tip identifies the direct basis for knowledge: what you saw or received, when it happened, where it happened, and how investigators can verify it. Preserve original messages, images, or files without editing them. Submit the information privately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do the Nancy Guthrie ransom notes prove she died?**

No. The released communication contains an unknown writer’s claim. Investigators have not publicly confirmed Nancy’s death, so responsible coverage must continue to describe her as missing.

**Why did authorities release the Nancy Guthrie communications?**

Authorities released them to generate leads, including the possibility that someone might recognize distinctive language or another meaningful detail. Release does not authenticate every statement inside a document.

**Can the public identify the ransom-note writer?**

The public can report a concrete recognition or direct fact, but should not publish handwriting comparisons, names, addresses, or accusations. Investigators possess evidence and context unavailable online.

**Where should evidence or tips be submitted?**

Preserve original files or messages and submit direct information privately through [tips.fbi.gov](https://tips.fbi.gov/) or by calling 1-800-CALL-FBI.
