A D.C. jury found Gerald Brevard III guilty on Tuesday, Oct. 27, 2026. It found him guilty on 16 counts. It also found that the crimes were bias-related. The bias was based on the victims' actual or perceived homelessness. [[1]](#src-1)[[2]](#src-2)[[3]](#src-3)

He has not been sentenced. Judge Neal Kravitz is set to sentence him on Jan. 29, 2027. [[1]](#src-1)[[2]](#src-2) The bias finding does not set that sentence. D.C. law lets the judge give a higher sentence. It does not make the judge do it. [[6]](#src-6)

## Who is this case about?

It is about Morgan Holmes. He was 54. He died on March 9, 2022, at his encampment on New York Avenue NE in Washington, D.C. [[1]](#src-1)

His family told WUSA9 in 2022 that he was a husband, a father and a grandfather. They said he served in the Army. [[8]](#src-8) A minister who held a memorial for him said his life had purpose and dignity. [[8]](#src-8)

Two other men were shot in the days before. Both survived. [[1]](#src-1)[[2]](#src-2)[[3]](#src-3) We do not name them. The U.S. Attorney's Office does not name them, and we do not need their names to explain this verdict. [[1]](#src-1)

## What did the jury decide?

The jury found Brevard guilty on 16 counts. The U.S. Attorney's Office says they include: [[1]](#src-1)

- first-degree murder while armed
- assault with intent to kill while armed
- aggravated assault while armed
- mayhem while armed
- firearm offenses

The release does not list all 16 counts one by one. [[1]](#src-1)

The jury also found the crimes were bias-related, based on the victims' perceived or actual homelessness. [[1]](#src-1)[[3]](#src-3)

Prosecutors say the attacks happened in Northeast D.C. between March 3 and March 9, 2022. [[1]](#src-1) That is the government's account. The verdict is the jury's finding. We have not seen the verdict form.

## What is the difference between a verdict and a sentence?

A verdict says if a person is guilty. A sentence is the punishment. They come at different times. Here, the jury gave the verdict on Oct. 27. The judge has not yet given a sentence.

## What is a bias-related crime in D.C.?

D.C. law has a name for it: "bias-related crime." D.C. Code § 22-3701 defines it. It is a crime, such as assault or murder, that shows the accused's prejudice against a group. The law lists the groups. Homelessness is on the list. [[4]](#src-4)

The law says "actual or perceived." That means it can count even if the attacker only believed the person was in the group. [[4]](#src-4)

The law also says prejudice does not have to be the only reason for the crime. [[4]](#src-4)

## Is a D.C. hate crime its own charge?

No. The U.S. Attorney's Office says D.C. has no stand-alone hate crime. The bias law is added to an underlying crime, such as assault or murder. [[6]](#src-6)

Prosecutors must prove the underlying crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The crime must also be tied to the person's prejudice. [[6]](#src-6) The jury found that here. [[1]](#src-1)

This case was tried in D.C. Superior Court under D.C.'s own law. [[1]](#src-1)[[4]](#src-4)[[7]](#src-7)

## Does the bias finding set the sentence?

No. D.C. Code § 22-3703 says a person found guilty of a bias-related crime can be fined and jailed for up to 1½ times the most the law allows for the underlying crime. [[5]](#src-5) That raises the top limit.

It does not pick the sentence. The U.S. Attorney's Office says the law "allows, but does not require, a judge to impose a higher sentence." [[6]](#src-6)

We do not predict what sentence Judge Kravitz will give.

Yes. The time to ask for a new trial or to appeal has not run out. As of Oct. 28, 8:43 PM PT, we found no new-trial motion or notice of appeal in the public sources we checked. We could not view the full court docket.
