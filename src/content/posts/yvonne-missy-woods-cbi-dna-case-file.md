There is no body in this case. There is no crime scene. There is a laboratory, a spreadsheet, and roughly a thousand criminal cases that nobody can now be certain about.

Yvonne "Missy" Woods spent nearly thirty years as a forensic scientist at the Colorado Bureau of Investigation, handling DNA evidence in more than ten thousand criminal cases. On June 23, 2026, she pleaded guilty to four felonies — cybercrime, first-degree perjury, attempt to influence a public servant, and forgery — under an agreement stipulating a **mandatory prison sentence of 8 to 16 years**. Roughly a hundred other counts were dismissed.

She will be sentenced on **Tuesday, September 8, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. Mountain Time** in Jefferson County District Court. A full day has been set aside for statements.

## Why the September 8 Hearing Matters

The plea fixed a range, not a number. Assuming the court accepts the agreement, Judge Poland decides where in the eight-to-sixteen-year band Woods lands — and a full day has been reserved because of who is expected to speak.

The people with standing to address the court in this case are unusual. They are not the family of one victim. They are survivors whose sexual assault cases were closed on a lab report that said no male DNA was found when male DNA was present. They are people whose convictions now rest on evidence nobody can vouch for. Their statements are the closest thing this case has to a reckoning, because a prison sentence — even at sixteen years — does not reopen an investigation that was closed years ago on a result that was wrong.

## What She Actually Did

This is where most coverage goes wrong, in both directions, so we are going to state it precisely.

According to the CBI's Internal Affairs report and the district attorney's charging release, Woods:

- **altered and deleted quantification values** — the measurement of how much DNA is present in a sample
- **re-ran entire batches of DNA multiple times without documenting it**
- **concealed possible contamination**
- **omitted material facts** from official criminal justice records
- **concealed her activities from technical review**, the lab's own peer-check process built to catch exactly this
- and in **more than 30 sexual assault cases**, submitted reports stating **"No Male DNA Found"** when male DNA was in fact present

### How it worked

The technical language above hides a simple sequence, and the sequence is the case.

Forensic DNA testing runs in stages. A sample is extracted, then **quantified** — measured to establish how much human DNA is actually present and, in a sexual assault case, how much *male* DNA is present in a sample dominated by the victim's own. That quantification figure drives what happens next: whether there is enough material to carry forward, how much to use, and how the eventual result should be interpreted.

Alter or delete the quantification value and you have moved the decision point to somewhere nobody else can see it. A sample that should have been flagged as containing male DNA can instead be recorded as one not worth pursuing. Re-run a batch several times without documenting the earlier runs and only the last run survives in the file; the ones that disagreed with it are simply gone. And the control built to catch this is **technical review**, in which a second qualified analyst re-examines the data behind a conclusion. Technical review can only check a conclusion against the records in the file. It cannot check it against records that were deleted.

That is how a report comes to read "No Male DNA Found." Not because an instrument said so, but because the record of what the instrument said had been edited before anyone else looked.

### The intern

The discovery deserves more than a line on a timeline, because of who made it.

Anomalies in Woods's data were identified in September 2023 through an **intern's research project** — someone at the bottom of the organizational chart, looking at the data without the assumption that a thirty-year analyst's files were fine. The lab's own peer-review process had been running against this work for years. A coworker raised concerns in 2014. She was accused of data manipulation in 2018, removed from casework, and reinstated. None of that stopped it. A student project did.

### What the records do not establish

**The CBI Internal Affairs report states that CBI found no evidence that Woods falsified any DNA matches.** The same report found no evidence of fabricated DNA profiles.

That matters, and it cuts against the intuitive version of this story. The established misconduct is the destruction and concealment of data, and the reporting of results the underlying records did not support — **not the manufacturing of a match against an innocent person**. No official record in this case establishes that Woods framed anyone.

### Why that is not a defense

The harm runs in two directions, and neither is small.

**False negatives.** Telling more than thirty sexual assault survivors that there was nothing to find, in cases where there *was* something to find, means perpetrators may never have been identified. Those are investigations that stopped because a report said stop. That is not a technicality.

**Reliability collapse.** Even where nothing was fabricated, work that cannot be trusted cannot support a conviction. In at least one case, independent retesting produced results inconsistent with Woods's original conclusion, and a murder conviction was vacated.

## The Cases

**1,045 cases** have been identified as affected, as of an August 2025 update. That figure grew over time — 654 in the June 2024 Internal Affairs report, then "over 800," then 1,003, then 1,045 — because it reflects an expanding review, not competing claims. Her work from 1994 to 2008 was still under review when the IA report was issued.

Denver Police independently expanded their own review past 1,300 cases — a separate figure from the survivor count further down, which happens to be similar.

And here is the number that says the most: as of December 2025, state prosecutors had requested only **14 retests**.

Roughly a thousand flagged cases. Fourteen requests. The Denver Gazette put it in a headline in March 2025 — hundreds of cases affected, very few retests ordered. Whatever else this scandal is, it is also a story about what happens after the scandal: the institutional energy to identify a problem substantially exceeded the institutional energy to fix it case by case.

### The Michael Clark case

Clark was convicted in 2012 of first-degree murder in the 1994 shooting death of Marty Grisham in Boulder. Woods performed the original DNA testing and concluded the DNA was Clark's — evidence that led to his arrest and conviction.

Independent retesting by an outside laboratory found inconsistencies with the DNA belonging to Clark. On April 11, 2025, Boulder County District Judge Nancy Woodruff Salomone **vacated the conviction**, and Clark was released on bond after serving more than twelve years of a life sentence.

**He has not been exonerated.** In September 2025 the Boulder District Attorney announced the office will retry him. In May 2026 the defense filed a 72-page motion to dismiss, arguing that a 32-year delay violates due process — dead witnesses, lost evidence — and that alternate suspects were never investigated. We could find no record resolving that motion, and no trial date had been set.

A court found the evidence no longer reliable enough to sustain the verdict. That is what "vacated" means. It is not a finding that Clark is innocent, and it is not a finding that Woods framed him.

**Clark's is the only vacatur we could confirm.** Reporting notes that petitions are being filed and that defense attorneys are overwhelmed, but no published tally exists of post-conviction petitions filed, granted, or denied. We are not going to assert a number.

These rows measure different things — appropriations, and one estimate of harm — and should not be added together.

| What | How much |
|---|---|
| General Assembly appropriation to CBI in response | **$7.4 million** |
| HB25-1275 appropriation, FY 2025-26 | **$3.1 million** |
| — to the Judicial Department | $688,316 |
| — to the Department of Public Safety | $1,418,145 |
| — to the State Public Defender and Alternate Defense Counsel | $1,000,000 |
| District attorney's estimated fiscal cost of her conduct (an estimate of harm, not an appropriation) | **$11,071,486** |

The legislature's own fiscal note contains the most quietly devastating figure in the case. Of roughly 1,003 affected cases, it estimated that only about **100 fell within existing post-conviction time limits** — leaving roughly **903** that could only be reached if the legislature created a new pathway. That is the state's own estimate of how many people had a potential claim and no door to walk through.

There is a second cost, harder to price. Diverting lab staff to review Woods's work pushed rape kit turnaround past 517 days against a 90-day goal. When it passed a separate bill, the legislature recorded a 554-day backlog affecting more than 1,300 survivors.

## What Failed

The most uncomfortable part of the official record is that this was caught late, twice over.

- **2014** — A coworker reported concerns about her testing methods. The conduct continued.
- **2018** — She was accused of data manipulation, temporarily removed from casework, and **reinstated**. The conduct continued.
- **September 2023** — Anomalies in her data were finally identified through **an intern's research project**.

Nine years after the first internal warning, an intern found it.

An independent assessment by Forward Resolutions, commissioned in January 2025 and released that July, found that past laboratory leadership "fell short in protecting the integrity and culture of forensic services," that leadership was "ill-equipped to handle crises and critical incidents," and that a productivity-focused approach had produced low morale under management described as autocratic and punitive. The report issued 52 recommendations.

### What Colorado changed

**HB25-1275 (Forensic Science Integrity)** established reporting requirements for crime lab employee misconduct, requires the lab director to investigate wrongful actions, mandates notification to district attorneys within 90 days of an investigation, and — critically — created a **post-conviction relief pathway** for defendants whose cases involved documented lab misconduct. That is the mechanism built to reach the roughly 903 cases outside normal time limits. It passed the House unanimously.

**SB25-304 (the Miranda Gordon Justice for Survivors Act)**, signed June 3, 2025, created a review board, requires law enforcement to notify victims every 90 days when DNA results are pending, directs labs to analyze kits within 60 days where feasible, and requires a public monthly backlog dashboard.

The most significant structural proposal — moving Forensic Services out of the CBI into a more independent structure within the Department of Public Safety, separating the laboratory from the investigative agency — is described in official materials as under consideration. We could not confirm it has been enacted.

## What to Watch Next

**Where in the range.** Eight years and sixteen years are very different answers to the same question. Watch what Judge Poland says about the survivors' statements when he explains the number.

**Restitution.** Whether any is contemplated is not something we could establish from the record.

**The Clark retrial.** The outcome of the May 2026 motion to dismiss is unresolved, and no trial date has been set.

**The records fight.** In February 2026 a judge ordered CBI to organize 5,900 DNA-testing documents into a searchable file for the defense. CBI appealed in May 2026. How that resolves determines whether other defendants can show a pattern in their own cases.

**The public dashboard.** SB25-304 requires the CBI to publish backlog data monthly. That is the one place a member of the public can watch whether turnaround times are actually coming down, without waiting for someone to write about it.

**The 1994 to 2008 window.** Her work from her first fourteen years was still under review when the Internal Affairs report was issued. We could find no announcement that it has finished.

**Whether the post-conviction pathway gets used.** HB25-1275 built a door for roughly 903 cases. Nobody has published how many people have walked through it. That number, when it exists, is the real measure of whether Colorado fixed this.
