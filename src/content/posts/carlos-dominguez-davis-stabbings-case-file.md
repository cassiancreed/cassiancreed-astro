This case is easy to describe wrongly, so we are going to be careful.

Over five days in the spring of 2023, three people were attacked with a knife in Davis, California. **David Breaux**, 50, was killed. **Karim Abou Najm**, 20, was killed. **Kimberlee Guillory**, 64, survived. Carlos Reales Dominguez, a former UC Davis student, was arrested and charged.

Three years and two trials later, there is **no murder conviction in this case**, and there will be no prison sentence. A jury acquitted Dominguez of first-degree murder in 2025, and in 2026 acquitted him of second-degree murder and of attempted murder, returning guilty verdicts on only two counts of involuntary manslaughter as lesser included offenses. The day after those verdicts, a judge found he had been **legally insane** at the time of the offenses — which means, under California law, that no conviction judgment and no sentence are entered against him at all.

What remains is a commitment hearing, and a genuinely difficult question about what happens next to a man whom three testifying experts diagnosed with schizophrenia and whom the court found legally insane, and against whom no judgment of conviction stands.

## Why the August Hearings Matter

Because of an unusual arithmetic problem.

When a California defendant is found not guilty by reason of insanity, the court commits him to a state hospital rather than sentencing him to prison. Penal Code § 1026.5(a)(1) caps how long that commitment can run: it is the longest prison term that *could* have been imposed for the offenses of which the person was convicted, including enhancements.

Here, the only guilty verdicts were two counts of involuntary manslaughter with weapon enhancements — among the least serious dispositions available in a case that began as a double murder prosecution. Dominguez has been in continuous custody since May 2023.

The § 1026.5(a)(1) maximum will be fixed by the commitment order. Coverage has put it at roughly six years and four months, and suggested time already served largely consumes it; that figure appears in no record we could read, and how pre-commitment custody is credited against the maximum is a genuine legal question. The order settles both.

What is not in doubt is the mechanism waiting behind it. If the state wants to hold him past the § 1026.5(a)(1) maximum, whatever that maximum turns out to be, it must petition under § 1026.5(b) for an extended commitment: two years at a time, renewable, and only on proof **beyond a reasonable doubt** that he represents a substantial danger of physical harm to others by reason of mental disease or defect. California courts have read that standard to require serious difficulty controlling dangerous behavior — a gloss from the case law under § 1026.5(b), not from the statute's own words.

The August 5 setting is described as the procedural step preceding the commitment order. Its precise scope has not been spelled out in any account we could find, and several major outlets do not mention that date at all. Victim impact statements are expected on August 19.

**One label to avoid.** Some coverage has called the August 19 hearing a "sentencing." On this record that word is wrong. No sentence follows an insanity finding.

## The People Who Were Attacked

### David Henry Breaux, 50

In Davis he was known as the Compassion Guy, and it was not a nickname anyone gave him ironically.

He graduated from Stanford in 1995 with a degree in urban studies. He grew up in Duarte, California, the son of a Jamaican immigrant mother and a French-Creole father who worked as a janitor and is described in reporting as abusive.

In 2009, after a relationship ended and after reading Karen Armstrong on compassion, he moved to Davis and gave away his possessions. He described the decision himself: "I had decided to spend the rest of my life with a pen and notepad and asking people to share their concept of compassion." He stationed himself at Third and C Streets — the corner locals came to call Compassion Corner — and asked people passing by to write down what compassion meant to them. He did that for fourteen years and collected entries from more than 11,000 people. In 2013 the community built the Compassion Bench at that corner. He later ran a community-funded Compassion Tour, studying the idea in cities around the country.

He was unhoused from roughly 2008 onward, largely by choice, staying with friends and couch-surfing. He had accepted shelter housing shortly before he died.

Davis Mayor Will Arnold described him as "gentle and kind, soft-spoken and thoughtful, brilliant and selfless," and "an icon to the core of our community." UC Davis Chancellor Gary S. May wrote that Breaux "led a life with real purpose, to connecting humanity for the greater good." His sister Maria called him "the sweetest, most gentle, most chill person I knew."

He was killed in Central Park, less than fifty yards from his own bench. The bench became the memorial.

There is one more thing on the record that belongs here. In 2016, Breaux left written instructions that if he were ever harmed, people should forgive the person who harmed him and help others do the same. His sister has publicly committed to honoring that. Breaux had spent years working toward forgiving his own father.

The David Breaux Memorial Scholarship, administered by the Yolo Community Foundation and funded by a local couple, supports Yolo County students who show financial need and a commitment to social justice and compassionate service. A 2026 recipient has been named.

### Karim Majdi Abou Najm, 20

He was born October 29, 2002, in Lebanon, the son of Majdi Abou Najm — a UC Davis professor — and Nadine Yehya, and brother to Jana. He attended International College in Beirut, moved to the United States in 2018, and graduated from Davis Senior High with honors.

At UC Davis he was a fourth-year computer science major in the University Honors Program, six weeks from graduating, and he had already built more than most people build in a career. He won HackDavis in both 2021 and 2022. He held a Provost's Undergraduate Fellowship and was inducted into Phi Beta Kappa and Phi Kappa Phi. He mentored other undergraduate researchers and helped onboard them into Dr. Lee Miller's Speech Neuroengineering and Cybernetics Laboratory. With Dr. Miller and a fellow honors student, Lara Ibraheem, he co-founded Cornische, a platform to connect faculty running funded studies with undergraduates looking for research work.

He was also working full time as a software engineer while finishing his degree. Since March 2023 he had been at XRAI Glass, building augmented-reality glasses that display live subtitles for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. He had interned at Audible and held a full-time offer there, and was planning a move to New York.

His father described him not by any of that, but this way: "Karim was a bundle of energy. A free spirit, someone who wanted to see goodness around him."

He was riding his bicycle home through Sycamore Park when he was attacked. It was a route his family traveled every day.

UC Davis awarded his degree posthumously, and his father walked in his place at commencement. The city named the Sycamore Park bike path the Karim Abou Najm Pathway, and a public artwork — *Fiat Lux*, in steel, glass and stone — was installed there. His family established two funds in his name: a memorial undergraduate research award and a change-makers prize, both still running in the 2026 cycle.

### Kimberlee Guillory, 64

She was stabbed through the wall of her tent in an encampment near Second and L Streets on the night of May 1, 2023, and was taken to UC Davis Medical Center in critical condition. She survived.

**The jury acquitted Dominguez of the attempted murder count concerning her.** There is no conviction and no adverse finding of any kind arising from that attack. Any account of this case that leaves that out is telling it wrong.

She has spoken for herself twice, and both times are on the record.

The first was from a hospital bed in **May 2023** — days after the attack, and three years before any jury heard the case. Speaking to a reporter then, she said her friends in the nearby tents came when she screamed: "We thought that we would be safe all together." Of the arrest, she said, "I'm glad that they got him. I was praying for that, that nobody else will have to go through this." And: "Me and the other two victims never did anything to him. There was no reason for him to come after people that he didn't even know."

Those were her words in 2023. In 2026, a jury acquitted on the count concerning her. Both of those things are true, and the second does not cancel the first.

The second time she spoke was in a courtroom. She testified in person at the 2025 trial, and became tearful when her own 911 call was played for the jury.

In that same 2023 interview she described her circumstances. She was 64 at the time. She had been unhoused for about two and a half years, since leaving her home to care for a daughter with pancreatic cancer; her daughter died. She stayed unhoused in part because shelters would not take her dog. And she said: "I'm grateful that I'm alive and that I wasn't killed."

Victim impact statements are expected at the August 19 hearing. Whether she gives one is hers to decide.

## What Actually Happened in the Two Trials

### The first trial, 2025

Jury selection began April 28, 2025. After roughly five weeks of testimony and nine days of deliberation, the jury returned **unanimous not-guilty verdicts on both counts of first-degree murder** on June 27, 2025, and deadlocked on the remaining counts — second-degree murder as to each decedent, and attempted murder. The Davis Enterprise, the Davis Vanguard and CapRadio all described the deadlocks as leaning toward acquittal; the per-count splits those outlets give do not match each other, so we have not printed them. A mistrial was declared on the hung counts.

Because of those acquittals, first-degree murder was off the table permanently. The retrial proceeded only on second-degree murder and attempted murder.

### The retrial, 2026

Jury selection began May 18, 2026; opening statements were May 28. Prosecutors argued cannabis-induced psychosis; the defense argued undiagnosed severe schizophrenia. The distinction mattered: under California law, a psychosis brought on by voluntary intoxication is treated very differently from a mental disorder.

On July 21, 2026, after nearly three days of deliberation, the jury acquitted on both second-degree murder counts and on attempted murder, and returned guilty verdicts on **two counts of involuntary manslaughter** as lesser included offenses, finding the personal-use-of-a-deadly-weapon enhancements true.

### The sanity phase

Under California law, a defendant who pleads both not guilty and not guilty by reason of insanity gets a bifurcated proceeding: sanity is *conclusively presumed* during the guilt phase, and litigated only afterward.

Both sides waived a jury on the sanity question and submitted it to the court. (Coverage of who did the waiving is inconsistent — some outlets credit prosecutors, others the defendant. Under the California Constitution a jury waiver in a criminal proceeding requires the consent of both the defendant and the People, so the accurate description is that both sides waived.)

The standard is Penal Code § 25(b). As written, it requires a defendant to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he was incapable of knowing or understanding the nature and quality of his act *and* of distinguishing right from wrong. In *People v. Skinner* (1985), the California Supreme Court read that "and" as a drafting error and construed it disjunctively — so either incapacity is enough.

The hearing lasted about fifteen minutes. Three mental health experts had diagnosed schizophrenia and were prepared to testify that Dominguez could not appreciate the nature, quality, or wrongfulness of his acts. Judge McAdam found him legally insane, saying the evidence "was overwhelming at trial." He ordered an evaluation by CONREP — California's Conditional Release Program, which assesses people committed after an insanity finding — and the case was set for a commitment hearing.

Melinda Aiello, speaking for the Yolo County District Attorney's Office, said afterward: "Violent cases involving schizophrenia, or other serious mental illnesses, are both complex and challenging." She added that while the outcome was not what her office hoped for, "we respect the role of the jury in our justice system."

## The Part That Confuses Everyone

**Is he convicted of involuntary manslaughter, or not?**

Both halves of the answer are true and neither works alone.

The jury *did* return guilty verdicts on two counts of involuntary manslaughter, and those verdicts were never vacated or overturned. But because he was then found legally insane, **no conviction judgment was entered and no sentence will be imposed**. The disposition is a hospital commitment under § 1026, not punishment.

The guilt-phase verdicts survive for one narrow statutory purpose: § 1026.5(a)(1) uses the word "convicted" to fix the maximum commitment term. That is the work those verdicts are still doing — setting a ceiling, not establishing criminal liability.

There is a corollary a lot of readers miss, and it cuts the other way. An insanity finding is not a finding that nothing happened. It presupposes the acts occurred and asks a different question — whether the person could know what he was doing, or know that it was wrong. That is why "found innocent" is not available language here either. He was acquitted of murder. He was not exonerated of the deaths.

So: do not write that he was "convicted of involuntary manslaughter" without immediately saying that the insanity finding means no judgment or sentence was entered. Do not write that the verdicts were "overturned" or "thrown out" — they were not. And do not write that he was found innocent.

## The Racial Justice Act Findings

Judge McAdam found **three violations of California's Racial Justice Act** by the prosecution over the course of this case. Only one is documented in any detail publicly.

On July 16, 2026, the defense filed a petition alleging that during closing argument the previous day, the prosecutor had improperly emphasized Dominguez's nationality, his status as a first-generation student, and his English-language background. Courthouse News retrieved the specific line: describing Dominguez joining a predominantly Filipino cultural fraternity as a freshman, the prosecutor said, "I would say that was the first bad choice that he made" — characterizing the fraternity as prioritizing marijuana use over academics.

McAdam found this was the third RJA violation by the prosecution in the case. From the bench: "To come in and say that a Filipino cultural fraternity prioritized cannabis over academics is impermissible on the face of it." He indicated a prosecutor need not use an overt racial slur to violate the statute.

The remedy was targeted rather than drastic. He struck the challenged portions of the argument and admonished the jury: "You are not to give it any weight; you are not allowed to discuss it during your deliberations." He reminded jurors that the cannabis evidence went only to mental state, not to character or credibility. Closing arguments then resumed and the prosecutor was permitted to continue. There was no mistrial and no dismissal.

The first two violations involved the prosecution's use of peremptory challenges against one Black prospective juror and one Hispanic prospective juror. McAdam said those were later remedied. Beyond those two sentences, the public record on them is essentially empty — no dates, no motion history, no description of the remedies.

One technical note is worth flagging rather than glossing. Discriminatory peremptory challenges in California criminal trials are governed by Code of Civil Procedure § 231.7, a different statute from the Racial Justice Act, with its own objection procedure and its own remedies. The two protect different interests: § 231.7 protects the prospective juror from being struck on a protected ground, while the Racial Justice Act reaches bias or animus directed at the defendant. Reporting describes the judge as counting all three as Racial Justice Act violations. Without the minute orders we cannot tell whether that reflects the court's actual legal basis or a shorthand used in the courtroom, and we are not going to assume.

We have written separately on [what a Racial Justice Act violation means](/post/what-a-racial-justice-act-violation-means-dominguez/).

## What to Watch Next

**The extended-commitment petition.** This is the real question. If prosecutors petition under § 1026.5(b), they must prove dangerousness beyond a reasonable doubt, and the commitment is renewable in two-year increments. That process — not the criminal case — now determines how long Dominguez is held.

**The maximum term, and how custody credits apply to it.** Both belong in the commitment order. That document is the thing to read.

**Which hospital.** The August 19 hearing decides placement. He was previously restored to competency at Atascadero State Hospital, but no facility has been designated for this commitment.

**The shape of the whole thing.** Step back and the arc is its own story: found incompetent to stand trial, restored at a state hospital, tried twice, acquitted of every murder count, found legally insane, and now facing a civil commitment that renews in two-year increments for as long as the state can carry its burden. That mechanism has no ceiling. It can hold a person longer than the criminal charges ever could have, and it is decided in hearings almost nobody attends.

**How the RJA findings are memorialized.** Three violations in a single prosecution is a substantial finding. Whether written orders become available — and whether the two peremptory-challenge violations are ever described publicly — will determine whether this case becomes usable precedent or stays a footnote.
