When Montgomery County detectives sent crime scene evidence from the Leslie Preer case to Othram in September 2022, the case had been cold for more than two decades. Leslie, 49, was found dead in her Chevy Chase, Maryland, home on May 2, 2001. They turned to a method called investigative genetic genealogy. Many people misunderstand it.

This post explains what happened in the investigation and how the method works. It is based on police, prosecutor and news reports.

The basics first. Othram is a forensic DNA lab in The Woodlands, Texas. It says it works with human DNA from trace amounts of degraded or contaminated material. In this case, Othram's case page says its scientists used what it calls Forensic-Grade Genome Sequencing to build a "comprehensive DNA profile" of the unknown man. Othram then gave that profile to investigators so they could run a genetic genealogy search. (The sources we found do not say whether this sample was degraded.)

The next step is investigative genetic genealogy, or IGG. This is where most public confusion begins.

IGG is not a search of the whole public. ABC News describes it as comparing a crime scene profile with "public genetic databases which law enforcement is permitted to access." In this case, charging documents cited by Bethesda Magazine say the profile was submitted to FamilyTreeDNA's database. Fox News reports that the key relative had voluntarily submitted her DNA to an online database. The sources we found do not name any other database used in this case.

The database returned a list of DNA matches, not a suspect. Charging documents say that list included the surname Gligor. A Montgomery County detective told ABC News that such lists can include "thousands of people," some of them very distant relatives. These were not suspects. They were relatives.

ABC News reports that the closest match led to a family line in Romania. State's Attorney John McCarthy said the DNA led to a distant relative from Romania. Using the family trees they built, detectives determined that Eugene Gligor was a first cousin twice removed of that match, according to ABC News. Gligor was also a name already in the original case file. He had dated Leslie Preer's daughter years earlier. A former neighbor's tip, dated Jan. 30, 2002, suggested he may have been involved. That was about nine months after the killing, according to charging documents cited by Bethesda Magazine. A tip is not proof. Police say he was 44 when he was arrested in June 2024. Othram's case page says he was about 20 when Leslie was killed.

What the database returned and who was finally named are two different things. That is how IGG works. The database gives biological leads. People then build family trees and narrow the pool. Investigators then test those leads with ordinary police work.

Which brings us to the confirmation step.

Once investigators had reason to look closely at Gligor, they needed proof that the crime scene DNA was his. ABC News reports that because his DNA had never been entered into a law enforcement database like CODIS, detectives had to collect a sample of his DNA to compare. The genealogy result was a lead, not proof.

In June 2024, with help from customs officials at Dulles International Airport, detectives collected a water bottle Gligor drank from and threw away during a secondary screening, according to ABC News and Bethesda Magazine. Fox News, citing court filings obtained by The Washington Post, describes the screening as staged. Police say the DNA from the bottle was compared with the crime scene DNA and "generated a positive match."

That match is what made this case. The genealogy database and the family tree were essential steps, but his arrest rested on a direct DNA match between the bottle and the crime scene. The genealogy work got investigators to the right person's water bottle.

Gligor was arrested in June 2024 and charged with first-degree murder. He pleaded guilty to second-degree murder on May 7, 2025, in Montgomery County Circuit Court. He was sentenced on Aug. 28, 2025, to 30 years with all but 22 suspended, plus five years of supervised probation.

For cold case investigators, the lesson is clear. IGG can produce leads when a person's DNA was never entered into a law enforcement database. If a relative has chosen to share DNA in a database open to police, that relative can become a bridge. The genealogist builds the bridge, the detective crosses it, and a direct DNA match confirms the result.

McCarthy called it the first time familial DNA was used to solve a cold case murder in Montgomery County. The method was new for the county. Othram's lab built a usable DNA profile from evidence collected in 2001.

The genealogy was the lead. The direct DNA match was the proof.
