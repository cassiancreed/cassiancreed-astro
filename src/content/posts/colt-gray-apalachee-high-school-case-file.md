Four people were killed at Apalachee High School in Winder, Georgia, on the morning of September 4, 2024: two teachers and two fourteen-year-old students. Nine more were injured. On July 24, 2026, the former student charged in the shooting, Colt Gray, pleaded guilty to all 55 counts against him in Barrow County Superior Court. The plea was non-negotiated — no sentence had been agreed in advance, and none has been imposed. Chief Judge Nicholas Primm accepted the plea, began the sentencing hearing the same day, and adjourned it to the following Monday.

That hearing resumes on **Monday, July 27, 2026, at 9:00 a.m. Eastern**. What happens there decides the only question the plea left open: whether Gray serves life with the possibility of parole, or life without it.

## Why the July 27 Hearing Matters

A guilty plea usually ends the argument. This one did not, because it was non-negotiated: Gray pleaded to everything without securing anything. Every count stands admitted, and the entire sentencing decision belongs to the judge.

Judge Primm stated the range in open court. The floor is life with the possibility of parole. The ceiling is life without parole. Nothing between those two points is available on a malice murder count under Georgia law, which provides that a person convicted of murder shall be punished by death, life without parole, or life imprisonment. The death penalty is not available here because of Gray's age at the offense.

So the hearing is not about guilt. It is about one word — *parole* — and about arithmetic.

The word first. Under Georgia law, "life with the possibility of parole" for a murder committed in September 2024 does not mean release. Murder is a serious violent felony under O.C.G.A. § 17-10-6.1, and a first conviction carrying a life sentence requires a **minimum of 30 years served** before the State Board of Pardons and Paroles may even consider parole. That minimum cannot be reduced by earned time or any other sentence-reducing measure. Parole eligibility is a hearing, not an outcome.

Now the arithmetic. The murder counts are only eight of the 55. The remaining counts — 18 of cruelty to children in the first degree, plus multiple aggravated assault and aggravated battery counts — carry terms of years, and a judge may run them consecutively. A life-with-parole sentence stacked behind decades of consecutive terms is a different sentence than a life-with-parole sentence standing alone. Watch the structure, not just the headline.

Behind all of it is a body of constitutional law about children. In *Miller v. Alabama* (2012), the U.S. Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids a sentencing scheme that *mandates* life without parole for juvenile offenders, and required sentencers to give individualized consideration to youth — immaturity, vulnerability to negative influence, and capacity for change. Georgia's scheme here is discretionary rather than mandatory, so *Miller*'s outright prohibition is not what governs; its individualized-consideration requirement is. And a later decision narrowed what that requires: in *Jones v. Mississippi* (2021), the Court held that a sentencer imposing life without parole on a juvenile homicide offender need **not** make a separate factual finding of permanent incorrigibility, so long as the scheme is discretionary. A reader watching Monday for the judge to make some formal finding about capacity for change should know the Constitution does not require one.

## The Four People Who Were Killed

### Cristina Irimie, 53

She taught math at Apalachee, working with English-language learners — students carrying two loads at once, the subject and the language it was taught in. She was born in Romania, in the Transylvania region, and taught there before emigrating to the United States in the mid-to-late 1990s. At St. Mary's Romanian Orthodox Church in Dacula she led a children's traditional Romanian dance group and volunteered in the kitchen.

She and her husband, Dorin, had no children of their own. A relative, Gabrielle Buth, told Atlanta News First that Irimie had put it plainly: "She said you know what I'm just going to have my children to dote on at school. My students are my children." Two nights before the shooting, she baked a cake and brought it to class so she could celebrate her birthday with her students.

Her pastor, Father Nicolae Clempus, described her this way: "The first thing you see is her big smile. She was always smiling." The head of Apalachee's math department, Jordan Rushing, said simply: "She definitely had a gift to work with young people."

Her funeral was held September 14, 2024, at Hamilton Mill Memorial Chapel in Buford, the Romanian Orthodox service moving between English and Romanian. In June 2025 her name was added to the National Memorial to Fallen Educators at Emporia State University.

### Richard "Ricky" Aspinwall, 39

He taught math and was the defensive coordinator of the Apalachee Wildcats. He came from Rome, Georgia, and had coached at Haralson County Middle School, Mountain View High in Lawrenceville, and Dunwoody High before Apalachee hired him in 2023 — head coach Mike Hancock made him the first phone call when he assembled his staff.

He and his wife Shayna, who also teaches in the school system, had two young daughters. We are not naming them.

Hancock talked about him the way you talk about someone you watched at the end of every practice: "That smile of him walking over to get the hugs — to get the daddy hugs, man, that's where I'm heartbroken. He loved being a girl dad, and he loved his wife." His coaching mantra was three words: effort, attitude, trust. A former colleague, Nick Bach, said, "You never had to ask Ricky to do anything, ever." A former player, Marquel Broughton, said, "For me specifically, he believed in me when I didn't believe in myself."

Students who were in his classroom told ABC News that when banging was heard on the lockers, Aspinwall opened the door and was shot in the doorway, and that a classmate pulled him inside before they barricaded it.

More than 300 people gathered for his celebration of life at Flowery Branch High School's stadium on September 8, 2024. A fundraiser for Shayna and the girls reached nearly $380,000 within days. He was added to the National Memorial to Fallen Educators alongside Irimie.

At the July 24, 2026 hearing, Shayna Aspinwall told the court: "It is a life sentence of grief and trauma for those left behind." She asked that her husband's killer not be given an opportunity for parole.

### Mason Alexander Schermerhorn, 14

He was born May 27, 2010, a freshman. He was autistic.

He loved Walt Disney World and LEGOs, played Roblox and Genshin Impact, and named Kingdom Hearts his favorite game. He had recently picked up the trumpet because he wanted to be like his sister Alanna. His favorite color was red, and when his family held his memorial service at the Jefferson Civic Center on September 14, 2024, they asked mourners to wear it. Hundreds did.

His sister said, "He loved everyone." A neighbor, Tommy Pickett, had watched him grow from an inquisitive boy into a teenager who always seemed to be smiling and laughing.

His mother, Breanna Schermerhorn, spoke at the July 2026 hearing. She said Mason "made people that were typically unseen feel seen, accepted and safe," and told the court she believed her son would likely have befriended the shooter had he known him. She also told the court she does not forgive him.

### Christian Gabriel Angulo, 14

He was a freshman, the son of Ismael and Emma Angulo. He liked video games and soccer. The people who knew him described a kid who was funny, free-spirited, and set on making other people laugh.

His sister Lisette wrote, "He was a very good kid and very sweet and so caring. He was so loved by many," and later, "We miss my baby brother so much. Honestly, we don't know how we're going to navigate life without him." His mother Emma told Univision about the last morning: "He gave his father and me a hug. I will always carry that in my heart."

His funeral Mass was celebrated on September 20, 2024, at Saint Matthew Catholic Church in Winder, in English and Spanish, before roughly 600 mourners, with Archbishop Gregory J. Hartmayer presiding. Community organizations brought pupusas and tamales. The procession moved through downtown Winder with people lining the sidewalks, and he was buried at Hoschton City Cemetery. He was the last of the four to be laid to rest.

## The Nine Who Were Injured

The Georgia Bureau of Investigation confirmed nine injured — eight students and one teacher. Reporting written after the full trial testimony in Colin Gray's 2026 trial indicates seven of the nine were struck by gunfire, with the others injured otherwise.

There is no comprehensive public accounting of how all nine are doing now, and we are not going to invent one. What is on the record is that the injuries ended a musical career and more than one athletic one, that at least one student needed roughly a year of surgeries before regaining the ability to walk, and that at least one stopped returning to school altogether. We have not made those descriptions more specific than that, because in a school this size specificity is a name by another route.

Several of the wounded have testified in open court under their own names or identified themselves publicly. Others have not. We are not naming any of them here.

## What the Plea Established

Gray was indicted by a Barrow County grand jury in October 2024 and prosecuted as an adult. The indictment ran 55 counts and was under seal until District Attorney Brad Smith read it aloud at the plea hearing.

Of those 55 counts, the categories confirmed on the record are four counts of malice murder, four counts of felony murder, and 18 counts of cruelty to children in the first degree, along with multiple counts of aggravated assault and aggravated battery. A published count-by-count itemization of all 55 is not available, and we are not going to reconstruct one by arithmetic — that would be a guess wearing the clothes of a fact.

His trial had been moved to a courthouse in Columbia County, near Augusta, and was scheduled to begin in October 2026. The guilty plea disposed of the indictment and mooted that trial.

## What to Watch Next

**Parole eligibility, and the stack behind it.** Judge Primm has said the choice on the murder counts is life with parole or life without. Under *Jones*, no separate incorrigibility finding is required, so what an appellate court would eventually review is whether youth was genuinely considered — not whether a particular sentence was recited. Watch equally for whether the terms of years on the other counts run concurrently or consecutively, because that is what decides whether a 30-year parole eligibility date means anything.

**Whether the felony murder counts merge.** The indictment charges four counts of malice murder *and* four counts of felony murder for the same four people. Whether the felony murder counts are vacated or merged by operation of law when judgment is entered is a routine Georgia sentencing question, but no source has addressed it in this case, so we are flagging it rather than predicting it.

**Whether an appeal follows.** A guilty plea sharply narrows the grounds available on appeal, but a sentence of life without parole for a fourteen-year-old offender is exactly the kind of sentence that draws one.

**The civil claims.** Several families have filed *ante litem* notices — Georgia's statutory prerequisite to suing a government entity. Those are notices, not lawsuits, and no court has ruled on any of the allegations in them. That distinction matters and we will keep making it.
