## The Woman Before the Case

Jane Hart was born in Ohio in 1906 to a Croatian mother who had immigrated the year before. Her childhood ended early. As the Journal & Courier's reporting reconstructed it, when her father died the family could not hold together: one brother was kept at home, another was sent away, and Jane was placed at St. Joseph's Orphanage for Girls in Cleveland, where she lived until she was old enough to support herself.

She moved to Chicago as an adult and built a quiet, working life as a housekeeper in Rogers Park, near Paulina Street and Lunt Avenue. The physical record kept the truth of that life: when investigators examined her decades later, they noted her calloused hands.

In the 1970s, Jane Hart disappeared from every public record. She was sixty-nine years old. No one reported her missing — because the family separated at that orphanage door had lost track of her so completely that some of her relatives never knew she existed.

## October 8, 1976 — Otterbein, Indiana

During the fall harvest, farmer Norman Skoog was running his combine through a field six miles north of Otterbein when he nearly struck a cardboard box sitting about fifteen yards off County Road 200 South. Inside, wrapped in plastic, was the body of a woman who had been shot in the back of the head.

Investigators concluded the box had been in the field for only about twelve hours — it was dry despite recent rain — but that the woman had been killed roughly a week earlier and transported some one hundred miles from her last known address in Chicago.

Investigators worked the leads the 1970s gave them. The mastectomy scar pointed toward a hospital record somewhere; the popular double-knit pantsuit pointed toward a retailer; the perfume bottle was the only personal item she was left with. Dental X-rays were compared against missing-persons files and fingerprints were processed by the Indiana State Police. Nothing matched. In a community where everyone knew everyone, no one knew her.

She was buried in an unmarked grave at Fowler Cemetery in Benton County. For the next four decades she was the Benton County Jane Doe — NamUs case UP58250.

## 2019–2021: The Case Reawakens

The modern investigation began with Benton County Coroner Matthew Rosenbarger, who grew up in the area and treated the case as a torch to be carried for the original investigators. In June 2019, her remains were exhumed from Fowler Cemetery so that a usable DNA profile could be attempted with modern methods.

In 2021, the Coroner's Office partnered with the DNA Doe Project, the volunteer nonprofit that applies investigative genetic genealogy — the discipline of combining crime-scene or unidentified-remains DNA with public genealogy databases and traditional records research — to name the nameless.

Extracting a genealogy-grade profile from remains buried for over forty years required a pipeline of specialists: Lakehead University's Paleo-DNA Laboratory and HudsonAlpha Discovery on early assessment and processing, Astrea Forensics on degraded-sample extraction, Intermountain Forensics on whole-genome sequencing, and bioinformaticist Kevin Lord translating raw sequence data into files the genealogists could work with.

## The 1.6 Percent Problem

When the genealogy began, the team hit the case's defining obstacle. Jane's ancestry was Croatian — and people of Eastern European descent are significantly underrepresented in the DNA databases available to investigative genetic genealogists. Her highest match shared just 1.6% of her DNA: roughly the genetic distance of a third cousin, a thread most searches could not follow.

"We could tell that our Jane Doe had Croatian ancestry, which posed a challenge," team co-leader Harmony Vollmer said, noting that without more people of Eastern European descent choosing to upload to databases like GEDmatch, FamilyTreeDNA, and DNAJustice.org, cases like this one remain among the hardest to solve.

What DNA alone could not finish, the paper record did. The team traced the distant match backward to a Croatian woman who immigrated to the United States in 1905 and had a daughter the following year in Ohio — then followed that daughter through census data, probate records, and institutional files, including the records of the orphanage where she was raised. "We uncovered further documentation from institutions that Jane had lived in, as well as probate records linking her to her family," said team co-leader Traci Onders. "This research revealed genetic and genealogical connections that enabled us to identify her as a candidate."

The candidate's life fit every fact the field had preserved: an Ohio birth in 1906, a Croatian mother, a Cleveland orphanage, a working life in Chicago's Rogers Park — and a disappearance from the public record in the very window the Benton County Jane Doe was found.

## Confirmation and a Family That Never Knew

Confirmation came through Jane's own family. Her niece, 78-year-old Mary Kay Ross, and a more distant relative, Nicole Cvetnić — a descendant of Jane's half-sister Helen, whose DNA was already in GEDmatch by her own choice — provided the reference samples. The Benton County Coroner's Office confirmed it: the woman buried at Fowler Cemetery was Jane Hart.

On July 15, 2026 — nearly fifty years after the discovery — the identification was announced publicly.

The confirmation carried its own quiet tragedy. Because the family had been fractured at that orphanage door in the 1910s, Jane's surviving relatives had not known she existed until the DNA Doe Project reached out. Her identification did not just close a case file; it restored a severed branch of a family tree.

"Identity is a basic human right," Onders said of the case. "Jane Hart, when she died, somebody went to great lengths to ensure that her identity was taken away from her."

## Where the Case Stands Now

Jane Hart's name is restored. Her killing is not solved.

Her homicide is unsolved. WLFI reported that her death remains an active homicide investigation; Coroner Rosenbarger, speaking to the Journal & Courier, was more careful — he said he was not certain of the case's formal status, but that any new information would be acted upon. What is beyond dispute is his public ask: the identification is an opportunity for anyone who remembers something — a name, a face, a connection to Chicago's Rogers Park in the 1970s, anything about how a sixty-nine-year-old housekeeper came to be left in an Indiana field — to finally make the record right. No suspect has been named, and nothing in the public record establishes who killed her or why. This page will state it plainly for as long as it is true: the person or persons responsible have never been identified.

If you have information about the death of Jane Hart, contact the Benton County Coroner's Office or the Benton County Sheriff's Office.

## Why This Case Matters Beyond Itself

Jane Hart's identification is one of the clearest demonstrations on record of two truths about investigative genetic genealogy.

First: a distant match plus a deep paper trail can do what neither can do alone. A 1.6% match — a third cousin's worth of shared DNA — became an identification because volunteers were willing to work census pages, probate filings, and orphanage records until the genetics and the documents named the same woman.

Second: the method is only as strong as the communities represented in its databases, and participation is a choice. The databases used in this work include only profiles their owners have explicitly opted in to sharing. That consent is the foundation the entire method stands on — and as the people who make that choice grow more diverse, more families like Jane's may be reunited with relatives they never knew they were missing.

For fifty years she was defined by where she was found. She isn't anymore. Her name is Jane Hart, and she is home.
