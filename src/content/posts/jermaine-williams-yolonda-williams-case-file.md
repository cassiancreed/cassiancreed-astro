Yolonda Williams was a social worker. She spent her working life helping people in Flagler County navigate crisis — including, specifically, domestic violence victims. She had celebrated her fiftieth birthday nine days before her husband killed her in their driveway.

On July 17, 2026, a Flagler County jury convicted **Jermaine Mandell Williams Sr.** of first-degree premeditated murder. They deliberated for 22 minutes. Five days later, after a two-day penalty phase, the same jury voted **11 to 1** to recommend a death sentence and found all four aggravating factors unanimously.

**No sentence has been imposed.** Under Florida law, a jury recommendation is not a sentence. Circuit Judge Dawn Nichols will impose sentence after a Spencer hearing, and as of today no date for that hearing has been published.

## Who Yolonda Williams Was

She was a care coordinator at Flagler Cares, a Palm Coast nonprofit, helping vulnerable people find their way through social services. Reporting describes her as a local advocate for people in difficulty, including domestic violence victims. Applying for food stamps, Medicaid, and Medicare Savings Programs — the unglamorous machinery that decides whether someone eats and sees a doctor — was the work.

She did not just work there. She built something there. Flagler Cares runs a community outreach program, twice a month at the Carver Center in Bunnell, offering behavioral health screening and care coordination. The organization's own description says the program "was initiated by our beloved team member, Yolonda Williams, to bring our services directly to the community."

Five weeks after she was killed, the Flagler Cares board voted to rename it. It is now the **Yolonda Williams Outreach**, and it still runs.

A client wrote to her workplace in February 2024, and they posted it: "I wish there was an Office Hall of Fame Employee Award because Yolonda would take that trophy home year after year, decade after decade — really there would be no competition! Yolonda's compassion and willingness to go above and beyond is beyond measurable."

She had five children. Her sister, Shawna Denise Hester, is now raising the two youngest.

She was, by her mother's account, someone with a quiet spirit and a brilliant mind who could cook, and who was not finished: she intended to write a book and to open her own business. What the book was about and what the business would have been are not on the public record, and we would rather leave that blank than fill it in.

Her mother told the court: "Her life mattered, her presence mattered, her voice, her laughter, her style, and her unmistakable swag — it all mattered." And: "She was my daughter, but she's also my true friend."

Her sister told the court, "Her death consumes me, literally eats me up."

Her aunt, speaking for all five children: "Yolonda was the heart of the family. Her smile brought comfort, her laugh brought joy, and her jokes brought light... Yolonda's absence is something we will never ever move on from." Her home, she said, was open for family gatherings and community events.

Eighteen people sat in the courtroom for her. The program she built is still open twice a month in Bunnell, and it still has her name on it.

## The Record of What Came Before

This is not a case where a history of abuse has to be inferred. It is in court filings.

One thing needs saying before the record does: leaving is the most dangerous moment in an abusive relationship, not the safe one, and protective injunctions are dismissed all the time — under pressure, under threat, or because a household with shared children and shared money cannot absorb the consequences of keeping one. That is context for what follows, not commentary on it.

In **January 2022**, Williams was convicted of **domestic battery, false imprisonment, and tampering with a witness**, and sentenced to 18 months of house arrest followed by probation. He was still on that probation in August 2024 — which is why he was charged with violation of probation on the day of the killing.

Also in 2022, Yolonda petitioned for a domestic violence injunction. In that petition she wrote that Williams "has been beating and terrorizing me since I was 13 or 14," and described repeated threats, including a gun put to her head more than ten times. She obtained the injunction, and it was dismissed about two months later.

Read against her age, that petition describes abuse beginning in adolescence and running roughly thirty-five years — a trajectory that starts in a teenage relationship and ends in an intimate partner homicide.

She used the system that exists. The courts had her petition, they had his conviction for battering her, and they had him on felony probation for it. She was killed anyway. The CDC's analysis of intimate partner homicides found that most occur at the victim's residence, and that a documented history of prior abuse is known in only about one in five cases — meaning the record usually holds *less* than what happened, not more.

## What the Jury Heard

The killing happened at about 8:14 a.m. on **August 2, 2024**, in the driveway of a Bunnell home. She was preparing to leave for work and had called her mother for a ride.

Prosecutors presented evidence, including surveillance and doorbell video, that Williams had **disabled her car by removing a fuse** so she could not leave, then went inside and retrieved a chef's knife. He stabbed her approximately twenty times. According to the same evidence, he stopped when his own father, driving past on the way to work, threatened him with a gun and held him until police arrived. Williams remained on scene and was taken into custody.

Her son, then 14, witnessed the attack and tried to intervene.

There is one more detail, and it only exists in the public record because of how she died. She cared for her nails religiously. It came out at trial because her broken nails were evidence that she fought.

Judge Nichols barred a heat-of-passion defense before trial, ruling that the defense had to first establish legally adequate provocation, and excluded a supporting defense psychologist's testimony as self-serving hearsay.

The jury — ten women and two men, with three alternates — took 22 minutes.

At the penalty phase the defense presented mitigation: Williams's father testified about his son's grief following the death of his mother weeks before the killing; his brother described their father's use of corporal punishment; and a defense psychologist testified about Williams's sense of himself as a man. The jury deliberated 1 hour and 38 minutes.

## What the 11–1 Vote Actually Means

Florida is the reason this vote is not a footnote.

From 2017 until 2023, Florida required a **unanimous 12–0 jury vote** before a judge could impose death. In April 2023, the legislature lowered that threshold. The statute now reads: "If at least eight jurors determine that the defendant should be sentenced to death, the jury's recommendation to the court must be a sentence of death." Fewer than eight, and the recommendation must be life without parole. At the time it passed, it was the lowest jury threshold in the United States.

So an identical 11–1 vote in this case, tried three years earlier, would have compelled a life sentence. Under current law it produces a death recommendation.

Two things still have to be true, and both are here. The jury must **unanimously find at least one aggravating factor, beyond a reasonable doubt**, to make a defendant death-eligible; this jury found all four unanimously. As reported, they were: a prior felony conviction with the defendant on felony probation; a prior violent felony conviction; that the murder was especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel; and that it was cold, calculated, and premeditated without pretense of moral or legal justification. (Those are the plain-English descriptions reported from the courtroom; no source published the subsection letters from the verdict form.)

**And the judge, not the jury, imposes sentence.** Judge Nichols may impose death **or** life without parole notwithstanding the 11–1 recommendation — the statute says the court "may impose" either. What she cannot do is go the other way: where a jury recommends life, the court "shall impose the recommended sentence of life."

## What a Spencer Hearing Is

The name comes from *Spencer v. State*, 615 So. 2d 688 (Fla. 1993), in which the Florida Supreme Court set out the procedure a trial judge should follow before imposing a capital sentence. It is a court-created step, not a statutory one.

At the hearing, the judge gives the defendant, defense counsel, and the State an opportunity to be heard; allows both sides to present additional evidence if appropriate; lets both comment on or rebut any presentence or medical report; and — specifically — affords **the defendant an opportunity to be heard in person**. The judge then recesses to weigh the sentence and, if imposing death, prepares written findings supporting it. Sentencing is set as a separate hearing afterward.

In plain terms: it is the last chance to put mitigation in front of the judge, and it is the judge's independent weighing step. The jury's role ended on July 22.

Reporting indicates the hearing is expected in August 2026. **No date has been set on the public record.**

## What to Watch Next

**Whether a Spencer date is set.** That is the next thing that will appear on any public calendar. Call the clerk before you travel.

**Whether Judge Nichols follows the recommendation.** She has the lawful authority to impose life without parole instead. If she imposes death, she must issue written findings, and those findings become the core of the automatic appeal.

**The automatic appeal, and which aggravator carries it.** A Florida death sentence goes directly to the Florida Supreme Court without the defendant having to ask. Nothing is appealable until sentence is imposed. If a death sentence is imposed, the judge must issue written findings supporting it, and those findings are the core of the appeal.

**What the supervision was.** He was on felony probation for battering her when he killed her. What that supervision actually consisted of, and whether anything in it was triggered by the 2022 injunction, is not something we could establish from the public record.

**The second count.** The indictment carried two counts — premeditated murder and aggravated assault with a deadly weapon. Every source we could reach reports only the murder conviction. Whether the jury returned a verdict on the assault count, and what it was, is not something we could establish. That requires the clerk's docket.
