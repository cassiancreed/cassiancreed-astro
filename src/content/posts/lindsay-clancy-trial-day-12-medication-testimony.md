Rebecca Jollotta’s testimony added important evidence about Lindsay Clancy’s reported symptoms, medication concerns, and clinical presentation before January 24, 2023. It did **not** decide whether Clancy experienced postpartum psychosis at another time, whether medication caused her condition, whether any provider committed malpractice, or whether she was criminally responsible under Massachusetts law.

Cora Clancy, 5; Dawson Clancy, 3; and Callan Clancy, 8 months, remain at the center of this case. Clancy has pleaded not guilty to three counts of first-degree murder. Prosecutors contend she acted deliberately and was criminally responsible. The defense argues severe postpartum mental illness and medication effects left her without criminal responsibility. Those are opposing trial positions, not findings by this article.

## What did Rebecca Jollotta testify about?

Jollotta, a psychiatric nurse practitioner who treated Clancy in late 2022, described treatment for anxiety, depression, insomnia, and severe distress. Reporting on her testimony described repeated medication questions and requests for changes, concerns about side effects and dependence on benzodiazepines, intrusive thoughts, and suicidal thoughts.

Jollotta testified that Clancy did not display psychosis during the encounters she described. She also testified that Clancy had not disclosed a plan to harm herself or her children during that treatment window.

Those observations matter because they are contemporaneous evidence from a treating professional. Their limits matter just as much: they describe particular encounters before the deaths, not a continuous observation of Clancy’s mental state and not the legal test the jury must apply.

## Did the testimony rule out postpartum psychosis?

No single treating encounter can answer the entire trial question. Jollotta’s testimony supports the narrower proposition that she did not observe psychosis during the sessions she described. It does not, by itself, establish or disprove Clancy’s mental state at the time of the children’s deaths.

The jury may weigh that testimony with records from other providers, expert opinions, witness observations, Clancy’s communications and writings, forensic evidence, and the judge’s instructions. A clinical label—or the absence of one in a particular appointment—is evidence to evaluate, not a verdict.

## What did cross-examination add?

Cross-examination focused on the limits of the information available to individual providers. Jollotta’s testimony did not supply a single unified record from every psychiatrist, hospital, and clinician involved in Clancy’s care.

That does not prove that the healthcare system failed, that a diagnosis was missed, or that anyone committed malpractice. It establishes a narrower evidentiary point: a clinician’s conclusion can be based only on the information available during the treatment relationship. The parties may argue different meanings from that limitation; the jury decides the criminal case.

## How is clinical testimony different from criminal responsibility?

Massachusetts criminal-responsibility law asks a legal question about capacity at the time of the conduct. In general terms, jurors consider whether, because of a mental disease or defect, a defendant lacked substantial capacity either:

1. to appreciate the wrongfulness of the conduct; or
2. to conform the conduct to the requirements of law.

Once lack of criminal responsibility is properly raised, the Commonwealth must prove criminal responsibility beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge’s final case-specific instructions control.

That is why “no observed psychosis” and “criminally responsible” are not interchangeable statements. Clinical testimony can inform the jury’s analysis, but it cannot replace the full legal inquiry.

## What did Day 12 testimony not prove?

Jollotta’s testimony did not independently prove:

- that Clancy did or did not experience postpartum psychosis at the time of the conduct;
- that medication caused or did not cause a particular mental state;
- that any clinician or institution committed negligence or malpractice;
- that the conduct was planned or unplanned;
- that either side’s complete trial theory is correct; or
- what verdict the jury should return.

Each of those questions requires other evidence, legal instructions, or a separate proceeding.

## Does a not-criminally-responsible verdict mean immediate release?

No. Massachusetts General Laws chapter 123, section 16 provides a court-supervised process. After an acquittal based on lack of criminal responsibility, the court **may** order hospitalization for observation and examination for up to 40 days. Any later commitment requires a petition, hearing, and the findings required by law. The statute does not support describing immediate hospitalization or long-term commitment as an automatic result in every case, and the verdict would not mean automatic release.

## Questions readers are asking

### Did Rebecca Jollotta diagnose Lindsay Clancy with postpartum psychosis?

The testimony covered Jollotta’s observations, treatment considerations, symptoms, and medication concerns. She testified that she did not observe psychosis during the encounters she described. The jury must evaluate that evidence with the rest of the record.

### Did no observed psychosis rule out psychosis later?

No. It establishes what the witness said she observed during particular encounters. It does not alone decide Clancy’s mental state at another time.

### Who must prove criminal responsibility in Massachusetts?

Once the issue is properly raised, the Commonwealth must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was criminally responsible.
